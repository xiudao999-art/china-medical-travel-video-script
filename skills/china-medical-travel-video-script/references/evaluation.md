# Test and improve the skill

Use this workflow when the user asks to evaluate or improve this reusable skill. Ordinary script requests need the normal release check, not a full experiment. Keep development records with the user's project, outside the installed skill's writing references, so future drafts do not copy test answers.

## Establish a comparison

1. Preserve the existing skill and one baseline output. Freeze the main brief: audience, format, topic, known facts, style, runtime, and CTA purpose.
2. Use a fresh writing agent with only that brief, the candidate skill, and applicable raw source materials. Do not provide the previous script, reviewer criticisms, or a preferred answer. If independent agents are unavailable, disclose that the test is a self-review and do not claim independence.
3. Give a separate reviewer the brief, candidate text, source material, and rubric. Hide version labels for a comparison where practical. Ask for quoted evidence, not unsupported praise or a rewritten answer.

For a broad optimization request, use a small suite that represents the intended uses: a spoken insight, local-interest story, repair after a mistake, practical explainer, an offer with supplied facts, and a timed dialogue where applicable. Evaluate each against its own format rather than demanding a story or all course principles in every piece. Inspect relevant course coverage separately from copy quality.

## Assess writing and factual integrity separately

First check for material factual inventions, fabricated testimony, unsupported clinical claims, cultural stereotypes, or failure to respect the requested format. Fix these before considering the piece publishable. Do not subtract a token penalty and let strong style compensate for a material false claim.

Then assess the six dimensions below on a 1–5 editorial scale: 1 serious failure, 2 substantial weakness, 3 usable with a conspicuous weakness, 4 strong with minor issues, 5 excellent with specific evidence.

| Dimension | Observable question |
| --- | --- |
| Opening and audience relevance | Does a prospective visitor have a reason to stay, and is the opening's promise fulfilled? |
| Specificity and progression | Does a response change what happens next, with a concrete action and payoff? |
| Spoken English and pace | Could a real speaker say this naturally in the available time? |
| Course fidelity and visitor agency | Does the scene respond to this person, leave silence open to interpretation, and demonstrate respect and reliability? |
| Service value and invitation | Can a viewer explain what help mattered, and is the next step relevant and easy? |
| Distinctiveness and coherence | Is there a memorable detail or insight, with no generic sermon or forced second service? |

A working acceptance threshold is at least 25/30 with no dimension below 4 and no material factual failure. Scores are subjective editorial judgments, not audience research or conversion evidence. Always retain the reviewer's reasons and dissent instead of reporting the score alone.

For timed dialogue, audit each scene's actual spoken words, explicit pauses, and actions that must happen in sequence. A scene can be risky without a text reviewer being able to prove it will overrun; distinguish a timing risk, an arithmetic mistake, and a measured failure. Do not call a small word-count discrepancy a substantive writing failure if it does not change feasibility.

## Revise the skill, then regenerate

Map each selected defect to the skill instruction or example that allowed it. Make the smallest change likely to improve other briefs. Correct explicit source omissions directly; add a general stylistic rule only when the problem repeats or the correction has a clear broader rationale. If the problem is simply a writer's isolated mistake, fix the draft without making it a universal rule.

Generate again from the frozen brief without showing the old draft to the writer. Review the new output on its own merits and compare with the baseline. Do not force the writer to recreate a sentence chosen by the evaluator. Avoid treating a preferred word or a high score on one script as proof that all scripts have improved.

## Check another topic and stop deliberately

Use at least one new brief not used to design the changes. For an extensive update, cover both local companionship and an informative role/scope explainer. These should not require the original waiting-room scene or a silence hook. Check for repeated openings, permission phrases, stock emotional language, and identical CTAs, as well as facts, natural English, and service value.

A new-topic result without a paired old-version output can show that the new script satisfies that brief; it cannot by itself establish an improvement or absence of regression relative to the old skill. For a test suite, report per-topic wins, ties, and losses. Retain a better earlier draft when appropriate, but do not present a curated best-of collection as if every item came from the same final skill run.

Default to at most three evidence-driven revision rounds. Stop once the main draft meets the working threshold and the new brief reveals no material regression. If two consecutive rounds show no meaningful gain or the round limit is reached, retain the strongest version and report the unresolved issue honestly. Continue beyond this only when the user requests it or a specific remaining material defect justifies another correction.

Deliver the usable skill, final script, and a brief account of what changed with evidence. Keep prior drafts and reviews available for inspection. Count words and estimate duration explicitly; do not call a text estimate a recorded read-aloud or call an AI review a human audience test.
