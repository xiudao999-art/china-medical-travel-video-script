# Source-derived story principles

This reference interprets two course materials for English content-marketing scripts. The first course includes a brief marketing analogy about a product's story, but neither source supplies a complete marketing method or medical policy. Audience strategy, hooks, CTAs, timing, claim checks, and editorial scoring are task-specific additions.

## Source 1: cross-cultural inclusion and trust

Local source: `/Users/mac/Documents/ChatGPT/文案写作skill/课程整理/跨文化沟通中的包容与信任_完整课程.md`.

- Sections 1 and 5 (00:33–01:55; 19:21–19:47; 22:48–23:57): participants discuss stories behind pictures and common ground; the lecturer uses a fabric's story as a marketing analogy and a hat to discuss identity. A real or clearly fictional interest, object, or personal meaning can make a video interesting. Use what matters to the individual; do not invent the host's memories or require every script to begin with a worry.
- Sections 2–3 (about 04:23–10:40): the “window” expresses willingness to see another person's world while retaining a “frame” of principles and boundaries. In a script, show a companion adapting to the visitor's preference without erasing their own role or promising unlimited help.
- Section 6 (about 24:13–28:02): trust rests on observable reliability and doing what one says; respect includes being able to hear the other person. The course presents trust, respect, love, and contribution as four pillars, but develops trust most fully. In a script, replace abstract “we care” claims with a small kept promise or clear handoff.
- Sections 7–8 (about 28:51–35:22): honesty, responsibility, keeping promises, and calm responses are discussed as trust-building behaviors. Avoid “fixing” someone or treating difficulty as weakness; show patient, competent support.
- Sections 3 and 8 (14:44–15:10; 34:53–35:22; 42:15–42:41): being wrong or finding expression difficult need not end the interaction. Show an appropriate repair after a small error, accept a visitor's correction, or offer time and encouragement so they can express their own meaning. Do not turn encouragement into pressure or speak for them.
- Section 10 (50:34–53:06): a leading-and-following exercise returns to calm, responsiveness, and reduced judgment. Adapt this as a responsive exchange; the transcript does not fully document the physical exercise, so do not invent its instructions.
- Course remarks and examples are not evidence for claims about any nationality, patient group, or hospital. Do not reproduce its cultural generalizations or uncertain transcription as facts.

## Source 2: silence and the patient conversation

Local source: `/Users/mac/Documents/ChatGPT/文案写作skill/课程整理/GC-3-SIlence.pdf` (16 pages).

- Pages 3 and 16 briefly recap identifying one's role and GATE (Greet, Ask, Tune In, Explain) before SPARK. Make who helps and what they can do clear when relevant. The full earlier lessons are not supplied; this recap does not require every marketing script to start with a self-introduction or reproduce all frameworks.
- Pages 2 and 11–13: silence can mean many things, including thinking, translating, emotion, uncertainty, or comfort. Behavior alone does not reveal its meaning. Let a pause breathe; never diagnose it. The deck explicitly treats culture as a clue, with the individual as the answer.
- Pages 5–10: SPARK is See → Prompt → Add → Relate → Keep. Notice behavior before inferring; invite without pressure; add reassurance or useful local information instead of asking question after question; follow the person's answer; sometimes continue with silence. This is a dialogue design tool, not required on-screen wording.
- Pages 14–15: the worked example uses a late doctor, a visitor looking at a screen, a factual update, and a specific offer to watch for their number. Its lesson is to make reassurance concrete. Do not copy the 20-minute estimate into a new script unless it is true for the filmed situation.
- Pages 15–16: participant feedback asks whether the visitor felt unpressured, interested in, listened to, and given enough time. The final exercise includes an intentional 3–5 second pause in a 90-second role-play. Use an actual pause when demonstrating silence, not as a compulsory device in every marketing video.
- Page 15 also offers five reflection prompts: safety, connection, challenge, creation, and contribution. They are distinct from the first course's four pillars; do not merge them into an invented single framework. Their writing application is to check whether a conversation gives space, follows the person, tolerates the pause, responds naturally, and makes the situation easier.

## Adaptation choices

SPARK's Add includes a small personal response and everyday conversation, not only administrative information. Its Relate follows what a person discloses. Preserve room for warmth, a specific feeling, and natural personality. The course sometimes uses inferential phrases such as “You must be tired”; this skill prefers a tentative check-in when the person's state is not known. That is an editorial adaptation, not a claim that the source bans the phrase.

The window metaphor helps writers stay curious without promising unlimited service. Calm should be conveyed through pace, listening, and dependable actions; do not turn the lecturer's remarks about “energy” into a clinical claim. The courses support communication principles, not medical-travel rankings, costs, treatment benefits, or universal facts about any hospital.

The first lesson's four pillars are Trust, Respect, Love, and Contribution, with trust developed most fully. Translate these into dependable action, being heard, ordinary human warmth, and useful help rather than demanding the words appear in every script. Common-ground comments and the chain linking friends, wealth, and contribution are classroom discussion, not proven causal claims. Transcription uncertainties and group generalizations are not evidence for a marketing claim.

## Story translation

| Course idea | Video choice |
| --- | --- |
| See the other person's world | Start with a visitor's specific uncertainty instead of a sweeping claim about China. |
| Trust is observable | Film one helpful action and its result. |
| Respect has boundaries | Offer choices and state where a clinician takes over. |
| Silence has multiple meanings | Use a short pause or listening shot, then a gentle check-in if needed. |
| Add, relate, keep | Make dialogue a response to the visitor, not a questionnaire or sales pitch. |
| A detail has a story or personal meaning | Let the viewer discover why something matters to this visitor; it can start from interest rather than distress. |
| Trust the person's ability to express and correct | Give them time or a manageable starting point, then let their words change the companion's action. |

## Worked example: 45-second social video

This is a fictional, illustrative scenario. The example does not assert a real service's capabilities.

| Time | Visual/action | Audio/dialogue | On-screen text |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 0–5s | Visitor pauses by a waiting-area sign with an illustrative appointment slip. | **VO:** “First appointment. Which waiting room?” | “Illustrative scene” |
| 5–13s | Companion and visitor compare the department on the slip with the sign; they match. | **Companion:** “This is the department on your confirmation. Here's the waiting area.” | “Check the practical details” |
| 13–24s | Visitor looks down at their notes. After their line, hold a three-second quiet beat before the companion replies. | **Visitor:** “I know we're in the right place. I'm just thinking.” **Companion:** “Take your time.” | No extra text during the pause. |
| 24–34s | Companion sits a little to the side, where the visitor can see them; leave the remaining beat quiet. | **Companion:** “I'll sit here while you think.” | “Room to think” |
| 34–45s | Return to the host; simple waiting-area image remains as context. | **VO:** “Help can mean checking the room, then giving you a quiet minute. What would help on your first visit?” | “What would help you?” |

The visitor's actual answer changes the response from wayfinding to space for thinking. The quiet beat illustrates this particular lesson; it is not a universal video template. Speech and the three-second pause fit the individual rows at a measured pace; timing remains an estimate until performed. Replace generic service phrasing with verified brand details when available.

For a city story, choose an independent starting point: someone is curious about how an object is made or wants to experience a neighborhood at their own pace. Let a disclosed interest lead to a concrete question or discovery, with the companion helping within the supplied scope. The visitor need not be confused or tired for companionship to have value. For an explanation video, a direct useful answer can carry the whole piece without any fictional scene. Address post-treatment suitability only when the brief actually includes recovery or activity limits.
