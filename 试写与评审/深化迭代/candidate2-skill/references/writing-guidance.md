# Writing guidance: English, scene choices, and concrete rewrites

Open the section that matches the script's problem. These are examples, not mandatory phrases or claims about the user's company.

## Sound like a companion, not a brochure

Write in natural international English that a mixed audience can follow. Use contractions when they fit the speaker. Prefer a concrete action to an adjective. Keep key nouns consistent: if the script begins with “appointment,” avoid switching to “consultation journey” for variety. Put complex medical terms in an approved clinical explanation, not casual escort dialogue.

| Weak line | Better direction | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “We provide a seamless, worry-free medical journey.” | “We'll show you where to check in and what to do next.” | An observable service replaces an unsupported emotional guarantee. |
| “Our professional team will solve every problem.” | “If a question is medical, we'll help you bring it to the doctor.” | States a useful role boundary. |
| “Don't worry. Everything will be fine.” | “We can check the next step together.” | Offers action without promising an outcome. |
| “You must be nervous.” | “Would it help to go over what happens next?” | Asks about a need without assigning an emotion. |
| “We take you to experience authentic Chinese culture.” | “Would you prefer a quiet tea stop or some time to rest?” | Lets the visitor choose; adapt the options to what is actually offered. |
| “Come to China for the best treatment and a perfect holiday.” | “If you're planning care in China, it helps to know who can guide the practical parts of the visit.” | Avoids treatment ranking and conflating care with leisure. |

Do not overuse “journey,” “warmth,” “care,” “empower,” “bridge,” or “peace of mind.” They can be valid when earned, but repeated abstractions make the video sound translated and promotional. Let the shot and action do part of the speaking.

Use emotion that the person has expressed. After “I'm worried I missed my number,” a relevant response concerns checking the number; after “I'm still thinking about what to ask,” it concerns giving them time. This is more useful than attaching “I understand” to every reply. A host can have a point of view and a memorable line, but a line such as “Trust starts with small things” should not replace the scene that proves it.

For an imagined interaction, make its status clear in the spoken setup (for example, “Imagine…” or “You might…”), then keep its tense and viewpoint coherent. Do not narrate fictional dialogue as “A patient told me yesterday.” Use “hospital companion,” “appointment support,” or a clear description for the audience when the label “patient navigator” sounds unfamiliar. Avoid using “escort” alone where it could be misunderstood.

A fictional story is only one format. A direct explanation does not need “Imagine,” characters, or a miniature drama. A visibly staged role-play can use a clear on-screen “Illustrative scene” label. If a host tells a fictional story as first-person experience, the spoken setup must also make its status clear. One unambiguous hypothetical cue normally suffices; repeating “fictional example” after a clear “Picture this” can interrupt the story. Place context and status where they remain clear while letting the viewer's reason to watch arrive early. Vary form for a useful reason, not by finding synonyms for the same opener.

Look for one human detail that changes the piece: why the visitor stopped at an object, what they want to ask, or what they corrected. A shared interest is useful only when supplied or clearly fictional; “we all love the same things” erases differences. A small response can be warm without an invented personal anecdote.

## Three usable story shapes

### 1. Hospital micro-story

**Viewer question:** “What happens when I arrive?” Open with a floor directory or check-in screen. The companion confirms the right place, asks whether the visitor wants to walk together, and explains the next logistical step. The visitor chooses. End with a CTA to ask about navigation support. Keep doctor or treatment claims out unless documented.

### 2. Helpful explainer

**Viewer question:** “Who answers my medical questions?” Host explains that the companion can help organize questions and navigate the visit, while the clinician provides medical answers. Demonstrate by showing a question written down and brought into a consultation. The value is role clarity, not a claim to interpret medicine.

### 3. Optional city companionship

**Viewer question:** “Can I see some of the city during my trip?” In a pre-appointment or other suitable context provided by the brief, start with the visitor's interest: a family member wants to understand a breakfast menu, or someone would rather explore a neighborhood than visit another landmark. Show the companion helping with one concrete detail and following the person's response. Rest is an available choice, not the mandatory ending of every story. If care has just occurred, do not imply an outing is medically suitable without confirmed guidance. A tour should not be the reward for treatment or a mandatory package step.

For awareness content, make the friction instantly recognizable. For consideration content, show process and limits. For action content, state what information the viewer should send to start a conversation, but only if that contact route is real.

## Conversation beats

Use one relevant question, wait for an answer, then respond to the answer. In-scene dialogue should not sound like a survey.

**Stiff:** “Where are you from? Is this your first visit? Are you scared? Do you want our tour?”

**More natural:** “Is this your first visit here?” [Visitor nods.] “The queue screen can be a little hard to read at first. Would you like me to show you where your number appears?”

If the visitor falls silent, the scene can hold a few seconds. The companion might say “Take your time” or ask a specific check-in question. The line should not tell the audience what the silence means. Use facial expression and pause responsibly; do not instruct an actor to perform a stereotype of a nationality.

For a misunderstanding, a short “Thanks for telling me; I misunderstood” followed by an adjusted action can carry more trust than a defensive speech. For expression difficulty, “Which part would you like to start with?” can help the person lead. Use such lines only when the scene calls for them; no universal apology or encouragement formula is needed.

## End with something earned

Check whether the ending would still fit an unrelated script. If so, it may be too generic. An explainer can end on a useful next step; an interest-led city story can ask about an actual preference; a service offer should name its real inquiry route and required information. After a scene has already demonstrated respect, adding “This is how we build trust” often weakens it. Keep an insight only when it adds meaning rather than repeating the plot.

For a role/scope explanation, preserve each necessary distinction in speakable language. Compress repeated statements that the role is “practical support” when the actions already establish that; do not remove the actual distinction between help forming a question and clinical interpretation. For a group of scripts, compare openings and endings together and adjust needless repetition. Do not demand novelty at the cost of accurate information or a natural line.

## Spoken pace and screen text

- Use the requested duration as an editing constraint, not just a word-count target. Check each shot as well as the total. Reading a sign, writing a note, confirming wording, and handing it over may need separate time even if the dialogue is short.
- At roughly 110–150 English words per minute, 30 seconds may fit about 55–75 words before substantial pauses; 60 seconds may fit about 110–150. Treat these as starting estimates and time a read-aloud draft.
- Leave the opening visual legible long enough for a viewer to understand the place and problem. Do not switch shots on every word.
- Keep designed on-screen text brief and useful: “Find the right department,” “Your choice,” “Ask the doctor.” Subtitles may repeat dialogue for accessibility, but do not overload the same frame with unrelated copy.
- Reserve enough time for the CTA to be seen and understood. Do not append three separate actions at the end.

## Editing common draft failures

| Symptom | Edit |
| --- | --- |
| The script sounds like a hospital advertisement | Replace general praise with one visitor problem and one visible companion action. |
| It says “we care” repeatedly | Show the team confirming, explaining, waiting, or following through. |
| The visitor is passive | Give them a preference, question, refusal, or decision. |
| The escort seems to give medical advice | Route that line to the clinician; let the escort help the question reach them. |
| The tourism beat feels opportunistic | Make it optional, later, and responsive to the visitor's interests and clinical guidance. |
| Dialogue feels like an interview | Delete extra questions and let the companion add useful information. |
| Cultural difference is treated as a rule | Replace nationality-based explanation with an observation about the individual and a respectful check-in. |
| The CTA requires an unverified channel | Use a generic invitation or ask for the real booking/contact path. |
| The host sounds like a staff trainer | Keep the visitor's experience foregrounded and remove explanations of the narrator's internal reasoning. |
| The scene promises an update but never delivers one | Show the checked answer or the honest next step; a claim of being reliable is not the update. |
| Several scripts sound interchangeable | Change the underlying human situation and form, not merely the city or adjective. Do not make every script use silence, a queue screen, “Would you like…,” or the same CTA. |
