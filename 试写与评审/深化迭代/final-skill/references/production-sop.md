# Production SOP: brief to ready-to-film English script

Use this for each new script and substantive revision. The gates are the decisions to settle; the exact number of scenes or words remains flexible. Do not print every internal worksheet to the user unless it helps review.

## Step 1 — Intake and define the job

Capture the following from the brief, prior context, or a stated working assumption:

| Field | Decision to make | If absent |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Audience | Who is deciding: prospective patient, family member, current visitor, or partner? What English familiarity? | Choose one plausible viewer and state it. |
| Viewer question or interest | What do they want to understand, decide, or experience? | Choose a practical uncertainty or a specific interest suited to the topic. |
| Journey moment | Before arrival, arrival, hospital visit, between visits, or optional leisure? | Select one scene, not the whole trip. |
| Funnel stage | Awareness, consideration, or action? | Use awareness for a broad brief. |
| Platform and format | Vertical social video, website explainer, interview, host-to-camera, etc.? | Use a 45–60 second vertical social script as a default, not a universal rule. |
| Duration | Spoken and visual runtime. | State an approximate runtime. |
| Offer and service scope | What can the team actually do, in which city, language, hours, and conditions? | Describe only generic patient navigation or local companionship. |
| Proof | Approved footage, documented process, named partner, review, patient consent, or data? | Use a dramatized routine action, labeled as illustrative when necessary. |
| Tone and brand | Calm, warm, practical, premium, humorous, etc.? | Use calm, warm, and competent. |
| CTA | What one step can the viewer take now? | Invite a question about support; do not invent a booking channel. |

Do not block routine drafting over a missing platform or tone. If a named institution, exact price, medical result, quoted testimonial, or other material claim is essential, ask for the source or write a safe version that omits it.

## Step 2 — Create a claim ledger

For every concrete statement likely to appear in speech, captions, or visuals, label it:

- **Confirmed:** directly supported by the user's approved service information or an authoritative supplied source; keep the exact scope and conditions.
- **Needs verification:** plausible but unsourced (e.g. “English-speaking escort,” “pickup at any airport,” “same-day appointment,” “insurance accepted”). Leave out of publish-ready copy or put in a clearly separate production note for checking.
- **Illustrative:** fictional visitor, staged dialogue, or hypothetical route. Keep it plausible; avoid presenting it as a real case or testimonial.

Also identify who is speaking and what supports the line. A host can describe the verified navigation service. A visitor character can express a fictional feeling in a labeled dramatization. Any general clinical statement needs an appropriate authoritative source; the companion must not give an individualized diagnosis or treatment recommendation. If a claim is central to the hook or CTA but unverified, rewrite the concept rather than decorating the claim with a disclaimer.

## Step 3 — Set the message before wording

Write one private sentence for each:

1. **Viewer concern or desire:** “I have an appointment, but I do not know what happens when I arrive,” or “I would like to understand the everyday life around me.”
2. **Useful answer:** “Someone can help me understand the next logistical step.”
3. **Visible proof:** “The companion confirms the department and shows where the queue number appears.”
4. **Change:** “The visitor knows where to go and what question to bring to the doctor.”
5. **One action:** “Ask what navigation support is available for my visit.”

Before drafting, consider two or three genuinely different openings privately: a recognizable moment, a useful question, or a specific observation. Select the one whose promise the scene can actually fulfill. A short script needs a reason to stay, not just a topic label. Do not output hook variants unless requested.

Use the course's story and common-ground material constructively: a known detail, object, interest, or personal meaning can give the viewer something to discover. In a fictional scene, the visitor may explain why a small experience matters to them. Do not invent the host's real history or assume a shared interest. For a series, vary the underlying form where appropriate: a direct answer, a small discovery, a repair after a mistake, or a demonstration. An explainer can prove value with useful steps rather than a manufactured dialogue.

Reject a concept if it tries to cover scheduling, treatment, prices, recovery, and sightseeing in one short video. For awareness, earn attention through a recognizable moment or useful answer. For consideration, show process, boundaries, and proof. For action, make scope and next step clear without inventing urgency.

## Step 4 — Outline filmable beats

Use a flexible sequence: **specific hook → context → companion action → visitor response or choice → modest payoff → CTA**. The order can shift for documentary or interview formats, but each beat should have a job.

- Open on the concern, useful answer, interest, or discovery that makes this video worth watching. A generic slogan, skyline, or logo rarely communicates that by itself. “Your appointment is booked. Which building do you go to?” works for wayfinding; an object that catches this visitor's attention can lead a city story.
- Put an observable service action early enough that the video is about what the team does. A smile alone is not proof.
- Complete the small action. If a character says they will check, let them return with the checked information or a transparent “I don't know yet” plus the next step. Do not invent actual institutional facts to supply this payoff; use a clearly hypothetical scene when the brief has no real case.
- In a character scene, give the visitor a meaningful contribution: a preference, correction, question, or nuance that affects what happens. Do not script them only to agree or thank the company. In a single-speaker explainer, respect their agency through practical options rather than inserting unrequested characters.
- Keep the payoff proportional: orientation, an understood next step, or a question passed to the right person. Do not convert an escort action into a recovery or treatment outcome.
- Let an optional city beat show preference, pacing, route, and a way to decline; do not use a landmark montage as a substitute for service proof.

Estimate pace before drafting. Use roughly 110–150 spoken words per minute as an editing range, adjusting for the intended speaker. Count the actual spoken words, excluding labels and stage directions. For each timed scene, estimate speech seconds as words × 60 ÷ intended speaking rate, then allow for planned pauses and actions that cannot overlap speech. A short first shot or a scene requiring writing and confirmation can fail even when the full-script average looks comfortable. Report an estimate unless a real timed read has been performed.

## Step 5 — Write dialogue and on-screen language

For hospital interaction, use the course-derived SPARK lens selectively:

1. **See:** Describe an observable behavior (“looking at the queue screen”), not a diagnosis (“nervous”).
2. **Prompt:** Ask one gentle, relevant question (“Would you like help checking the number?”). Avoid an interview chain of “Where are you from? Why are you here? How long are you staying?”
3. **Add:** Offer useful information, reassurance supported by what is known, or a small, honest personal response. A detail about everyday life or gentle humor may fit the person and moment. Do not answer a clinical question beyond the escort's role or invent the narrator's personal history.
4. **Relate:** Follow the person's answer rather than returning immediately to a sales line. Test whether your response would still work unchanged if their answer were different: if so, it may be generic. When the person says they are worried or tired, acknowledge what they actually disclosed. The restriction is against treating silence or nationality as evidence of a feeling.
5. **Keep:** Continue if invited; otherwise allow a pause. “Take your time” can be better than another question.

Write each spoken line as something a real person could say in one breath. Distinguish the host's explanatory voice from natural in-scene dialogue. A host may speak directly to the prospective visitor; avoid explaining every internal decision with “Now I have a need to respond to” or “First I observe.” Keep an intentional pause in a silence-focused demonstration, without requiring silence in every topic. A caption should add context, a keyword, or an action cue; avoid duplicating every spoken sentence in large on-screen text. Subtitle requirements, when requested, are separate from these designed captions.

For city companionship, follow an expressed interest with a concrete local detail and useful help: understanding a menu, choosing a route, or asking a craftsperson a question, if within the supplied scope. Keep location-specific facts supported. Use pre-appointment, family, or non-treatment time when the brief specifies it; address treatment-related activity limits only when relevant. Do not append a city sentence just to advertise both services. A series can cover the two roles across separate stories.

When a scene includes a misunderstanding or small service mistake, let the companion accept the visitor's correction, acknowledge their own part briefly, check the facts, and change the action. When someone struggles to express a question, offer time or a manageable starting point, then let them supply or confirm their meaning. These are ways to show calm and trust in the person's ability; do not write a permanently infallible guide or praise the visitor like a child.

## Step 6 — Draft the production script

For a complete production script, use table columns **time / visual and action / audio or dialogue / on-screen text**. For ordinary copy requests, present the English script as clean paragraphs first. Include speaker labels if there are multiple speakers. Mark pauses, ambient sound, or visual-only moments only when they carry meaning. Use feasible locations and props; do not require a real patient record or unauthorized hospital filming to make the concept work.

After the table, provide:

- one exact CTA line or end card;
- a short **verify before filming/publishing** list for facts, permissions, testimonial status, and service scope still uncertain;
- a clear **illustrative scenario** note when actors or fictional dialogue could be mistaken for a real case.

If the user asks only for voiceover, output voiceover first and add minimal production notes. If they request several concepts, keep each concept distinct in viewer question and proof, rather than changing only its opening line.

## Step 7 — Release check

Read the script as a prospective visitor and as the producer. Revise any failed item:

1. **Hook:** Is the first beat a specific concern, useful answer, or unexpected but truthful observation? Can a viewer understand it without the caption?
2. **Single message:** Can the video's promise be stated in one sentence? Remove detours.
3. **Visible proof:** Does the companion do something filmable and within the confirmed service scope?
4. **Agency and respect:** Does the visitor choose, ask, decline, or set a pace? Is silence left open to interpretation rather than diagnosed?
5. **Role boundary:** Are clinical questions directed to clinicians or qualified interpreters? Is any tour optional and compatible with confirmed guidance?
6. **Claim audit:** Does every factual assertion in speech, captions, graphics, and CTA have evidence? Are fictional cases and testimonials distinguished?
7. **English ear test:** Read it aloud. Fix literal translations, breathless lines, stiff dialogue, empty adjectives, or a question barrage.
8. **Time test:** Check the whole script and every timed scene. Budget silent beats, speaker changes, reading, writing, confirmation, and movement where they cannot happen together; shorten speech or simplify the shot when required. Do not solve a crowded shot only by claiming a faster natural delivery.
9. **Filmability and privacy:** Can the shots be obtained with available people, locations, consent, and redacted information?
10. **Ending:** Does the final line fit the purpose and this video's content? For an explanation, a usable step may be enough. For a story, a specific preference question may invite connection. For a supplied offer, state the actual inquiry action and preserve its conditions. Avoid restating the same moral and then appending a generic sales line. Do not invite disclosure of diagnoses, records, or private medical details in public comments.

For a revision request, keep the user's accepted message, facts, runtime, and format unless they ask to change them. Diagnose which gate failed, edit those lines and adjacent beats, then rerun the relevant checks. Do not rebuild an entire script for a small tone correction.

For an explicit request to improve the reusable skill, use [evaluation.md](evaluation.md); a polished one-off rewrite is not evidence that the skill now writes better.
